# Gemini Flash handles scores, not shots

**Short answer: not yet at human-tagger level for shot-by-shot charting, but cheap enough to be worth a two-week experiment, and the business case depends on a hybrid design rather than a Gemini-only one.** On the evidence gathered, Flash-tier Gemini models cost only about **$0.08 to $0.76 per hour of video at 1 fps** (my arithmetic from aggregator prices), so economics are not the obstacle; perception is. The only tennis-specific rally benchmark found, TennisTV, had the best closed model (GPT-4.1) at roughly **45% overall at 1 fps**, with Gemini 2.5 Pro close behind ([TennisTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15602v2)). No independent test of any current Flash model on tennis was found, and a ball crosses the court between 1 fps frames, so hits and bounces are largely invisible at default sampling. Dedicated trackers reach about 0.97 to 0.99 F1 on ball tracking ([TrackNetV5](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02789v1)), and the one published hybrid (a parser feeding a small Qwen3-VL-8B) reportedly beats GPT-5, Gemini and Claude ([TennisExpert](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13397v2)). My assessment is that Gemini Flash can plausibly reach tagger-level on the objective tier (score, point winner, aces, double faults, rally length, serve direction) and will fall short on the subjective tier (forced vs unforced, depth, shot type), where human charters themselves disagree. The Match Charting Project (MCP, 17,000+ matches, CC BY-NC-SA) gives free ground truth for the experiment, but its non-commercial licence, YouTube's ToS and EU copyright rules all constrain a commercial product built on broadcast footage. **Critical caveat: nearly every source below is a search-result summary, because primary sites (ai.google.dev, tennisabstract.com, arxiv.org, eur-lex) were unreachable from the research environment. Treat every number as unverified until you check the primary page; the verification table near the end lists what to check first.**

## How to read the confidence labels

Each claim carries one of three labels. **High** means a primary or near-primary page was read in full (this is true only for the MCP GitHub README, data dictionary and matches CSV). **Medium** means a search summary quoted a primary source or several independent sources agree. **Low** means a single aggregator, blog, vendor claim or my own inference. Inferences and arithmetic are marked as mine. Several model names and dates in the notes (Gemini 3.5 to 3.8, Gemini 4 Argon) postdate common training data and could not be cross-checked against Google's own pages, so they are Medium at best.

## Evidence says VLMs struggle on tennis, but hybrids work

The published evidence is thin and old. TennisTV evaluated 16 multimodal models at 1 fps "due to budget consideration"; the best closed model scored about 45% and both Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-4o miscounted hits in an example, which led the authors to say sampling density must be tuned per task ([TennisTV v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15602v1), [v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15602v2)). SPORTU's hard video tasks topped out near 58% for GPT-4o, with 2024-era models ([SPORTU](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08474v4)). VidDiffBench found models only slightly above chance on easy sports-technique comparisons ([project page](https://jmhb0.github.io/viddiff)). On temporal grounding, VideoZeroBench reports Gemini-3-Pro and 2.5-Pro at about 32% tIoU with most others under 20%, though Gemini received full video and audio while others got 1 fps ([VideoZeroBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01569)); another paper has Gemini-2.5-flash at 0.053 mIoU on hour-long grounding ([arXiv 2606.12300](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.12300)). A research football test found paid-tier Gemini 3 Flash timestamps matched real moments with no invented events but missed several shots ([BanyanBoard](https://banyanboard.com/blogs/sports-analytics/how-accurate-are-multimodal-ai-models-for-football-soccer-video-analysis-a-benchmark-study-of-gemini-and-qwen/), a non-peer-reviewed blog). Higher frame rates (16 fps vs 1 fps) improved several sports tasks ([arXiv 2503.13956](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.13956)). Confidence: **Medium** that general VLMs are weak at fine-grained tennis; **Low** that this applies to Gemini 3.x Flash, since none of these tested it on tennis.

Specialised computer vision is far ahead on the perception sub-problems. TrackNet's original tennis tracker had 84.3% F1 on one cross-validation setup ([TrackNet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03698)); TrackNetV5 reports F1 0.9859 and 114 FPS on a T4 ([arXiv 2512.02789](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02789v1)). Do not mix these with the TrackNetV3 badminton figures in the same literature. The best hybrid evidence is TennisExpert: a parser extracting scores, shot sequences, bounces and player locations feeds a Qwen3-VL-8B model trained on TennisVL (202 broadcast matches, 471.9 hours, 40,523 clips), and the authors claim it beats GPT-5, Gemini and Claude ([arXiv 2603.13397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13397)); I did not retrieve the score tables, so the margin is unknown. My inference: the structured parsing layer, not model size, is the differentiator, which implies Gemini should be the interpreter and not the sole timekeeper. No source covers amateur or handheld footage, which is the coach's real use case.

| Approach | Best evidence | Weakness | Evidence confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gemini/GPT zero-shot at 1 fps | About 45% on TennisTV | Misses hits and counts; no timestamp-error data | Medium |
| Gemini agentic video | Vendor: 88% fewer tokens on long-video QA | No tennis test; can skip segments | Low |
| Dedicated ball tracker (TrackNet family) | F1 0.97 to 0.99 on benchmarks | Benchmark footage, not amateur phone video | Medium |
| Parser plus small VLM (TennisExpert) | Reported to beat GPT-5, Gemini, Claude | Scores not retrieved; needs training and data | Low-Medium |
| Human charter (MCP style) | Used as ground truth | Subjective categories; no kappa published | Medium |

## Human taggers are accurate on facts and inconsistent on judgment

MCP charters type only the "1st", "2nd" and "Notes" columns in an Excel workbook; everything else (point winner, rally count, ace, double fault, forced and unforced flags) is derived from those codes ([MCP README](https://github.com/JeffSackmann/tennis_MatchChartingProject), [data dictionary](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JeffSackmann/tennis_MatchChartingProject/master/data_dictionary.txt)). Confidence: **High** (files read directly). Partial notation, from search summaries of the quick-start guide, is serve direction 4/5/6 (wide/body/T), f/b for forehand/backhand, "@" unforced, "#" forced, "*" winner, with error letters n/w/d/x; for example "4ffbbf*" ([quick start](https://www.tennisabstract.com/blog/2015/09/23/the-match-charting-project-quick-start-guide/)) (Medium; the full Instructions tab was not read). The README describes no validation process for submitted charts. Professional feeds differ: Sportradar scouts enter data live with a Live Data Entry team as failover and withhold service outcomes until post-match verification when scouts drop out ([Sportradar docs](https://developer.sportradar.com/tennis/docs/tennis-ig-live-match-retrieval)), but no accuracy or latency figures were found. No source gives human charting time per match; my inference is several hours for a 2 to 3 hour match (Low).

The key finding for target-setting: no published Cohen's kappa or percent agreement exists for tennis charters, but qualitative evidence says forced versus unforced is subjective. A study of 8,800+ MCP matches found charters agree on patterns that almost always yield one class and disagree in a grey zone ([charter-consistency study](https://ramizheman.github.io/mcp-charter-consistency), Medium, secondary). A FiveThirtyEight writer counted 21% more unforced errors than official Wimbledon tallies, and the club's spokesman called the labelling "very subjective" ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/wimbledon-is-very-forgiving-of-players-errors)). So "matching a human" has no fixed ceiling on the subjective tier. My proposed target tiers, with per-tier pass thresholds being my own judgment calls rather than sourced numbers:

| Tier | Stats | Why | Suggested pass bar vs MCP | Gemini-only feasibility (my estimate) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Objective | Point winner, score after point, ace, double fault, rally length (+/-1) | Determined by events or scoreboard | At least 95% points correct | Plausible with scoreboard OCR and a score state machine |
| 2 Semi-objective | Serve direction, shot type (forehand/backhand), net approach | Visible but fast | At least 80% | Uncertain; needs higher fps or tracker input |
| 3 Subjective | Forced vs unforced, depth, placement | Humans disagree | Report as agreement with a typical charter, no fixed bar | Unlikely to be reliable |

## Free ground truth: MCP is the best fit, with a licence catch

The MCP repository holds per-match metadata (`charting-{m,w}-matches.csv`), point files split by era, 16 aggregate stats files per gender, the data dictionary and the charting workbook ([repo](https://github.com/JeffSackmann/tennis_MatchChartingProject)). The matches header has 15 columns (match_id, players, handedness, date, tournament, round, time, court, surface, umpire, best-of, final-tiebreak flag, charter) and **no video URL**; example IDs encode date, tour, tournament, round and players, and entries run to at least May 2026 ([matches CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JeffSackmann/tennis_MatchChartingProject/master/charting-m-matches.csv)). The points files carry `Set1/Set2`, `Gm1/Gm2`, `Pts`, `1st`, `2nd`, `PtWinner` and `rallyCount`, with Player 1 always the first server ([data dictionary](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JeffSackmann/tennis_MatchChartingProject/master/data_dictionary.txt)). Confidence: **High** for these files; **Medium** for the 17,000+ match and roughly ten million shot counts (blog snippet, README is stale at "5,000+"). The licence is **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, attribution required, non-commercial only** ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JeffSackmann/tennis_MatchChartingProject/master/README.md)). Internal research evaluation is probably fine, but a commercial product cannot embed it or distribute derived sets without Sackmann's permission (my legal inference; verify).

Other sources are secondary. TennisVL (202 matches, 471.9 hours, 40,523 rally clips with commentary-style captions, shipped as YouTube URLs plus annotations) is the largest recent video-aligned set ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13397), [GitHub](https://github.com/LZYAndy/TennisExpert)), but its captions come from a research pipeline and are not independent human charts. TenniSet has five 2012 Olympic matches with timed events ([Adelaide](https://web4.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/113022)); Vid2Player has manually marked hit times for Wimbledon 2018 and 2019 ([arXiv 2008.04524](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.04524)). Licences for all of these were not retrieved. Sackmann's tennis_atp and slam point-by-point repos returned 404 via the fetch tool, so their status is unconfirmed. Alignment of video to chart is feasible: SmartTennisTV segments rallies and reads scoreboards with tennis-rule correction ([arXiv 1801.01430](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.01430)); for one 10-minute clip, align manually using the score on screen against the `Set1/Gm1/Pts` columns.

## Designing the self-improving loop without fooling yourself

Prompt optimisers exist and work, but tiny datasets overfit. GEPA reflects on failures and keeps a Pareto frontier of prompts ([arXiv 2507.19457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457)); DSPy's MIPROv2 tunes instructions and few-shot demos ([arXiv 2406.11695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11695)). Overfitting evidence is small-scale and mostly non-peer-reviewed: OPRO-style training accuracy often runs 5 to 20 points above test ([arXiv 2309.03409](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03409)), Decagon found 20 to 100 training samples best for GEPA ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/blog/optimizing-gepa-for-production)), and one 30-example run produced GEPA prompts at 34.0% versus 62.4% for the seed ([arXiv 2607.11944](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11944)). Treat these as cautionary (Low-Medium). Since the unit of independence is the match, not the 10-minute window, my design (inference) is:

1. Split by match, player and surface, never by window within a clip; freeze a test set of 20 to 30% of matches that neither you nor the optimiser sees until the end.
2. Use leave-one-match-out or k-fold on the rest for the optimiser's validation signal; cap iterations and early-stop on plateau; promote a candidate only if it beats the incumbent by more than seed noise across multiple seeds and does not regress a canary set of previously solved hard points.
3. Prefer deterministic fixes over prompt tweaks. A tennis score state machine (legal 0/15/30/40/deuce/advantage, tiebreaks, serve rotation and side alternation) validates every output and gives a **label-free signal**; on violation, retry with the error message and scoreboard frames.
4. Use a two-pass pipeline: a cheap pass segments points (serve, rally, dead time) and reads the scoreboard; a second pass extracts shots per point at higher fps, escalating only low-confidence points. Sample 3 to 5 outputs and escalate where votes split.
5. Use coarse temporal tolerances (0.5 to 1.0 s for shots, 1 to 2 s for point boundaries) because 1 to 5 frame tolerances in precise-spotting work ([arXiv 2604.22839](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22839)) are unrealistic at 1 fps; report a tolerance sweep (a 0.15 s tolerance and 0 to 0.5 s sweeps appear in broadcast event work, [arXiv 2610.38042](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.38042)).
6. Use Batch (50% off, up to 24 h) for optimiser runs and put the long stable prompt first to benefit from caching, which is reportedly a 90% discount on cached tokens ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/scale-your-ai-workloads-batch-mode-gemini-api/), [optimization docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/optimization)).

## Legal and ToS: private evaluation is manageable, a commercial product on YouTube footage is not

YouTube says its ToS bars unauthorised downloading and scraping, and its CEO called third-party scraping for AI training a "clear violation" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15509945), [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/04/04/openai-youtube-clear-violation-terms-service-ai-sora-training/)). The wording I could check came from archived 2016/17 ToS versions barring downloads unless a download link or written permission exists ([archived ToS](https://web-wp.archive.org/web/20170811164130/https:/www.youtube.com/t/terms)); check the current text at youtube.com/t/terms. Tool-distribution law is a separate matter: the 2020 youtube-dl takedown was reversed, which says nothing about whether using such tools breaches ToS or copyright ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/11/github-reinstates-youtube-dl-after-riaas-abuse-dmca)). In the EU, DSM Directive Art. 3 covers research-organisation text and data mining (TDM) and Art. 4 covers anyone with lawful access, including commercial use, but rightholders may reserve rights by machine-readable opt-out ([Kluwer](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-new-copyright-directive-text-and-data-mining-articles-3-and-4/)). Spain implemented this in Art. 67 of Real Decreto-ley 24/2021, which commentators say transposes imperfectly ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/esp/publication/royal-decree-law-24-2021-of-2-november), [EARE](https://eare.eu/eare-position-on-the-spanish-royal-decree-to-introduce-extended-collective-licenses-for-ai-training/)). Confidence: **Medium** on the framework; **Low** on how it applies to audiovisual works or to sending footage to a third-party API. Official ATP data is commercially licensed through Sportradar ([addendum](https://sportradar.com/official-atp-addendum/?lang=en-us)), so broadcast footage is layered with broadcaster, tournament and tour rights.

My risk reading (not legal advice): contract risk under YouTube's ToS exists independent of any copyright exception, and "lawful access" is arguable if copies came from a prohibited download. Spain has closed exceptions, not US-style fair use. A small, internal, non-redistributed evaluation set is lower risk than a product that redistributes clips or fine-tunes on them, and API-only prompting with your own labels is lowest. Whether Gemini accepts YouTube URLs directly was not verified; if it does, passing a URL avoids local downloads but not the ToS question. The cleanest path: your own recorded matches and coaching sessions (with consent; GDPR applies to identifiable people including minors, and Spanish image-rights law LO 1/1982 applies), written agreements with academies, or evaluation licences from rightsholders. Have a Spanish IP lawyer confirm before any commercial use.

## Recommended experiment plan

**Goal.** Decide in about two weeks, for under roughly $50 of API spend (my estimate), whether Gemini Flash can reach tier-1 accuracy and how far it falls short on tiers 2 and 3.

**Clips.** Choose 8 to 12 matches in MCP (check by `match_id`) whose official full replay is on YouTube, from different tournaments, surfaces and players, with a clear on-screen scoreboard, main-camera view and few replays. From each, cut a roughly 10-minute window starting at a game boundary and covering 2 to 3 games (about 25 to 40 points per clip), favouring long rallies and at least one break point. Read the chart rows first and confirm they match the screen. Add 3 to 4 clips of your own phone or fixed-camera footage, hand-charted by you in MCP notation, since that is the real product target and MCP cannot cover it. Keep the YouTube clips private and undistributed.

**Split.** Freeze 3 matches (about 25%) as the untouched test set; use the rest for development with leave-one-match-out validation. Keep 1 to 2 hard clips as a regression canary.

**Models and arms.** Run four arms on identical clips: (A) Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at 1 fps; (B) Gemini 3.8 Flash at 1 fps; (C) 3.8 Flash at higher fps via trimmed sub-clips if the API allows custom fps, else agentic mode; (D) the best of these plus scoreboard OCR and the score state machine. Add a pre-trimmed point-level variant for the best arm to separate perception from segmentation errors. If budget allows, one Pro-tier or Argon run as a ceiling.

**Metrics.** Report separately by tier. Tier 1: percentage of points with correct winner and correct score-after-point, ace and double-fault F1, rally-length error within one shot. Segmentation: point-boundary F1 at 1 s and 2 s tolerances. Tier 2: serve-direction accuracy and shot-type F1 with 0.5 s and 1.0 s tolerance via greedy matching, plus per-rally edit distance on the shot-code string. Tier 3: forced/unforced agreement with MCP, shown next to your own agreement with MCP on a re-charted sample as a rough human baseline (a sample of one is only indicative). Also log score-state violation rate, cost per match-hour, and run-to-run variance across 3 repeats. Success criteria I suggest: tier 1 at or above 95% on the frozen test set, tier 2 at or above 80%, and a clear statement of the tier 3 gap.

**Loop.** Baseline all arms; categorise errors (missed point, wrong winner, hallucinated shot, timestamp drift, scoreboard misread); fix deterministic issues in code first; then run GEPA or MIPROv2 on the development split using structured per-point diffs as feedback, early-stopping on validation plateau; accept a prompt only if it beats the incumbent beyond seed noise and does not regress the canary set; evaluate once on the frozen test set at the end. Decision rule: if arm D passes tier 1 and approaches tier 2, build a coach pilot; if shot-level detection stays below about 60%, test adding an open-source ball tracker (TrackNet family) and keep Gemini for interpretation, or stop.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question: Gemini Flash is probably not a replacement for a human tagger as a single end-to-end model, but at under a few dollars per match-hour it is a credible interpretation layer on top of scoreboard reading, a score state machine and, if needed, a ball tracker. The evidence that matters most is also the evidence that is missing: nobody has published Gemini 3.x Flash timestamp error or hallucination rates on tennis, and nobody has published human charter agreement, so both ends of the comparison are unmeasured. Your two-week experiment would produce both numbers cheaply, and the human-agreement baseline you generate on tier 3 may itself be a useful product positioning fact.

The commercial picture is narrower than the technology picture. The money in tennis data sits in betting and tour contracts that a coach cannot reach, SwingVision already owns the consumer app slot, and Spanish racket-sport growth is in padel. The opening, if it exists, is a Spanish-language, GDPR-aware coach workflow built on your own footage and academy relationships, where licensing is clean and your agency skills are an advantage. The legal exposure of YouTube broadcast footage and the non-commercial MCP licence argue for keeping them as internal test material and never as product inputs.
